Advancement

The path through Scouts BSA

Scouts advance by learning skills, demonstrating them, serving in leadership, participating in the troop, and living the Scout Oath and Law. Requirements can change, so use the current official handbook and BSA resources.

Scout

Learn the foundations of the program, including the Oath, Law, motto, slogan, sign, salute, handshake, patrol method, and basic joining skills.

Tenderfoot

Build beginning camping, fitness, first-aid, citizenship, and practical outdoor skills.

Second Class

Develop greater independence in navigation, cooking, aquatics, first aid, service, and outdoor participation.

First Class

Demonstrate the core abilities expected of a capable Scout, including leadership, camping, navigation, safety, and emergency skills.

Star

Earn merit badges, serve the troop in a position of responsibility, complete service, and continue active participation.

Life

Continue merit badges, leadership, service, and preparation for the capstone Eagle Scout journey.

Eagle

Complete required merit badges, leadership, service, an approved Eagle project, a statement of ambitions, and a board of review.

Use current requirements

Always verify details against the current Scouts BSA Requirements book and official national guidance.

Tradition

Symbolism of the First Class badge

The emblem combines symbols that remind Scouts of the Oath, service, freedom, knowledge, and cheerful duty.

Three points

The trefoil resembles the north point of a compass and represents the three parts of the Scout Oath: duty to God and country, duty to others, and duty to self.

Eagle & shield

The national emblem of the United States represents freedom and a Scout’s readiness to defend that freedom.

Two stars

The stars stand for truth and knowledge. They also suggest guidance at night and the outdoor life of Scouting.

Scroll & knot

The turned-up scroll suggests a cheerful smile while doing one’s duty and carries the Scout motto. The knot recalls the slogan: “Do a Good Turn Daily.”

Outdoor Ethics

Care for and share the outdoors

Our objective is to develop a culture of responsible outdoor choices that reduces the impact of Scouting activities and improves the experience of others.

The Outdoor Code

As an American, I will do my best to:

  • Be clean in my outdoor manners
  • Be careful with fire
  • Be considerate in the outdoors
  • Be conservation-minded

Leave No Trace

  1. Plan ahead and prepare
  2. Travel and camp on durable surfaces
  3. Dispose of waste properly
  4. Leave what you find
  5. Minimize campfire impacts
  6. Respect wildlife
  7. Be considerate of other visitors

Tread Lightly!

  • Travel responsibly
  • Respect the rights of others
  • Educate yourself
  • Avoid sensitive areas
  • Do your part

Outdoor Ethics Guide

This Scout position of responsibility represents outdoor ethics on the patrol leaders’ council, trains others, demonstrates advancement skills, helps plan conservation activities, supports recognition, and serves as a role model outdoors.

Adult advisors remove barriers, help with planning and execution, and guide evaluation while allowing the youth leader to lead.

A Scout Is Reverent

Religious emblems

Religious organizations have developed emblems programs that help young people grow in their faith. Scouting America recognizes these programs and permits approved emblems to be worn on the uniform, but each faith organization develops and administers its own curriculum.

Scouts should speak with their family, faith leader, or local council to identify the appropriate program and current requirements. Scouts respect the convictions of others in matters of custom and religion.
